# Discovery questions by assumption

A working set. Take the shapes, replace the content, and rewrite every `explanation` with the real context.

Order matters more here than in most studies: past behaviour first, solution last. Once the participant knows what you are hoping to hear, everything after it is contaminated — so put anything that reveals the idea at the very end, and read the earlier answers as the real data.

Field reference: `deutero-study-authoring/references/question-types.md`.

---

## Opening: establish the context (1 question)

**Q — the work (text, 3 turns)**

> "Tell me how [the relevant part of their work] actually runs at the moment. Start from what kicks it off."

> `explanation`: This is orientation and rapport, and it also tells us whether they are the right person at all. Listen for whether they own this or inherit it from someone else. Do not mention the product or the problem. If they describe a process nothing like the one we assumed, follow that — a wrong assumption surfacing here saves the whole study.

---

## Assumption 1: the problem exists

**Q — last occurrence (text)**

> "When did [the problem] last come up?"

> `explanation`: We want a date and a story, not a frequency estimate. "It's constant" is not an answer — ask for the most recent specific instance. If they cannot produce one, that is a finding, and a valuable one; record it and move on without pressing, because pressing produces a manufactured example.

**Q — frequency (choices)**

> Options: "Most weeks", "Monthly-ish", "A few times a year", "It's happened, but rarely", "I don't think this happens to us"

> `explanation`: Include the disconfirming option and mean it. A participant who picks the last option is the most informative person in the study, not a failed interview.

---

## Assumption 2: the problem is severe

Effort already spent is how you measure pain in an interview. Money already spent is better still.

**Q — what they did about it (text)**

> "What did you do about it that time?"

> `explanation`: The whole answer is here. Get the sequence, who else got involved, and how long it took. If the answer is "nothing, we just lived with it", that is a real and common finding — follow with what would have had to happen for them to act.

**Q — prior spend (multi_select + follow_up)**

> "Have you ever done any of these to deal with it?"
> Options: "Bought a tool", "Hired or assigned someone", "Built something internally", "Paid a consultant or agency", "Put it in a plan and it didn't happen", "None of these"

> `explanation`: Existing spend is the single strongest signal in the interview. On any pick, get what it cost, when, and whether it is still in place. "Put it in a plan and it didn't happen" is especially informative — get what displaced it, because that thing is your real competitor for their attention.

**Q — cost of the problem (text, 2 turns)**

> "What does it cost you when it goes wrong?"

> `explanation`: Take whatever unit they offer — hours, money, a missed deadline, an argument with another team. Do not push for a dollar figure they do not have; an invented number is worse than an honest "I don't know". If they give one, ask once how they arrived at it.

**Q — priority (ranking)**

> "Of the things on your plate this quarter, where does this sit?"
> Options: the problem plus four plausible competing priorities from the brief.

> `explanation`: A real problem that ranks fifth does not get bought. This question catches the most common false positive in discovery — a genuine problem nobody will act on. Probe the items above it.

## Assumption 3: the segment is right

**Q — who decides (choices + follow_up)**

> "If you did decide to buy something for this, who actually signs it off?"
> Options: "Me", "My manager", "A committee or procurement", "Someone in another team entirely", "We wouldn't buy for this"

> `explanation`: In B2B the user and the buyer are frequently different people with different answers. If it is not them, find out who and what that person cares about — that tells us whether we are interviewing the wrong population.

**Q — the trigger (text)**

> "What would have to happen for this to become something you dealt with this quarter rather than next year?"

> `explanation`: We are after the buying trigger: an event, a threshold, a deadline, a person joining or leaving. Triggers are what make a market reachable, and they rarely appear unless asked for directly.

---

## Assumption 4: solution shape — last, and only if needed

**Q — the current workaround in detail (text, 3 turns)**

> "Walk me through how you handle it now, step by step."

> `explanation`: The workaround is the competitor. Get every step including the manual ones and the tools it passes through. The steps they apologise for are the ones worth replacing.

**Q — approach comparison (choices + follow_up)**

> "If someone were going to fix this for you, which of these would fit how you work?"
> Options: three or four genuinely different approaches — one of them ours, described neutrally and not first.

> `explanation`: Present ours as one plausible option among real alternatives, never as the proposal. Probe the reason for the pick and, more usefully, the reason for the rejections. If they pick ours, ask what would have to be true for it to be worth switching from what they do now — switching cost is where most of these ideas actually die.

## Questions never to ask

| Don't | Why | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Would you use this?" | Hypothetical about a product that does not exist; costs nothing to say yes | "What do you use now, and what did it cost?" |
| "What would you pay for it?" | People cannot price something they have not used | "What do you spend on this today, and out of whose budget?" |
| "Do you think this is a good idea?" | Asks for an opinion of you, not of the problem | Ask about the last time the problem occurred |
| "How often do you usually…?" | Invites a summary, which is a guess | "When did it last happen?" |
| "Would it help if it also did X?" | Every added feature sounds free in the abstract | Make X one option in a ranking against real alternatives |
| Anything that names a benefit in the question | Tells them the answer you want | State the situation neutrally and let them name the benefit |

## Length and tier

Eight to twelve questions is a full discovery interview, roughly 20–30 minutes in chat. Participants can pause and resume, so length is less punishing than on a scheduled call — but attention still decays, so put the assumption you care most about second or third, not last.

Ask the user to choose the tier — never set it for them — and recommend `standard` or `premium` if the plan allows (`standard` needs Solo or above, `premium` needs Pro or above). Discovery lives entirely in the follow-ups, and the cheaper tier probes more literally. On Hobby, `open_weights` is the only option; compensate by enumerating more and writing much more detailed `explanation` guidance.
