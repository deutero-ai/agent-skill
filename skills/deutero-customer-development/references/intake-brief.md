# The discovery brief

What to get from the user before designing. Ask conversationally — this is a checklist for you, not a form for them. Two exchanges is usually enough.

## 1. The idea, in one paragraph

Who it is for, what it does, what it replaces. If they have a deck, a one-pager or a plan, read it and play back your understanding in two sentences before asking anything else — it saves an exchange and it surfaces misreadings immediately.

## 2. The customer

| Get | Adequate | Not adequate |
|---|---|---|
| Role or situation | "Ops managers at 3PL warehouses, 50–300 staff" | "Logistics companies" |
| A verifiable behaviour | "Build shift rotas weekly, currently in Excel" | "Care about efficiency" |
| Where they can be found | "LinkedIn, the r/logistics community, two industry Slacks" | "Everywhere" |
| Who pays | "The ops director; it comes out of the software line" | "The company" |

The verifiable behaviour is what makes the study buildable: it becomes the screening question, the recruitment targeting and the interview's anchor. Without it you cannot tell a qualified participant from a polite one.

## 3. The problem, as a cost

- What goes wrong today, concretely.
- What it costs — hours, money, errors, risk, stress. A number if they have one.
- **How do they know?** Distinguish "we interviewed twenty of them last year" from "it's obvious" from "this happened to me at my last job". The last is a legitimate and common source, and it is also a sample of one.

## 4. What people do today

The current solution, including the ugly one. "A spreadsheet", "a contractor", "an intern", "nothing". Push past "there's no solution" — there almost always is, and it is usually manual. If the honest answer really is nothing, the study changes: it now has to establish that the problem is felt at all, which is harder and needs more open questions and a lower expectation of clean findings.

## 5. The riskiest assumption

Ask directly: "What would have to be false for this to be a bad idea?" Then push once, because the first answer is usually the second-riskiest assumption — the comfortable one. The uncomfortable one is typically that the problem is real but minor, or that the people with the problem cannot buy.

Write the answer down verbatim. It becomes the study's `research_question`.

## 6. The decision

What happens on each outcome? Build, don't build, change segment, change the model, raise on it. If there is no decision attached, name that gently and ask whether to proceed — sometimes the honest answer is that they want reassurance, and it is better to have that conversation than to ship a study that cannot provide it.

## 7. Constraints

- **Timeline.** How soon do they need this? It decides whether you can recruit a real population or only reach their own network.
- **Budget for participants.** Incentives change who answers and how fast. Free recruiting skews toward people who like the founder.
- **Existing contacts.** A waitlist, a newsletter, past customers, a community they run. Enormously useful for speed and enormously biased; use it, and say so in the report.
- **Deutero plan.** Simulations and the standard/premium tiers need a paid plan; Hobby is 15 credits a month and open-weights only.

## Mining a deck or plan

Decks reliably contain: the customer description, the problem statement, the claimed cost, and the competitive set. They reliably do not contain: the riskiest assumption stated as such, the behaviour that makes a customer findable, or the evidence base behind the problem statement.

So read for items 2, 3 and 4, then ask for 5 and 6. Treat a market-size slide as background — TAM tells you nothing about whether one person has the problem — and treat the competitive landscape slide as your best list of what people use today, which is directly useful for the interview.

## When the brief is thin

Say which parts are missing, state the assumptions you are building under, and build the study anyway. Then name which findings will be unreliable as a result — usually anything about the segment, if the segment was vague. Scaling the work down is the user's decision, not yours; hiding the weakness is nobody's.
